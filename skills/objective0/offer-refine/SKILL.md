# Offer refine

Make an offer, a value proposition or a piece of copy more valuable to the person reading it, using Alex Hormozi's value equation and the methods in *$100M Offers* (with the useful parts of *$100M Leads* and *$100M Money Models*).

Each run gives the person two things:
- **Suggestions:** 5 to 10, each tied to one lever, with the reason.
- **A pushed rewrite:** the copy with every suggestion applied, written to go further than they'd likely send.

They tone it down from there. Nothing this skill writes is final.

The test for every suggestion: does it make this reader want it more, because it's more valuable to them?

## The equation

Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷ (Time delay × Effort and sacrifice)

Raise the top two, cut the bottom two. The bottom two are usually where the gains are: most copy already promises a big outcome and never says how fast or how easy. The moves for each lever, and the rest of the method, are in [references/frameworks.md](references/frameworks.md). Read it before suggesting anything.

## Before anything, ask

One question at a time, and only what you can't see in the copy:
1. **What is it, and where will it appear?** An invite, a landing page, a listing, a message, a job post.
2. **Who reads it?** A buyer, a partner, a candidate. What stage, what role, what they care about.
3. **How warm are they?** They know you, they know of you, or they've never heard of you.
4. **What do you want them to do?**
5. **What's real?** Prices, limits (seats, hours, dates), proof (numbers, names, quotes) and any guarantee you'd stand behind. Anything not given stays as `[N]` or `[TBD]`.

If there's no clear picture of the reader, build one first with the buyer step in [references/prompts.md](references/prompts.md): one specific person, with a name, a company, a stage and their own words.

## Mode 1: review a piece of copy (the usual one)

1. **Map what's already there.** For each lever, and for scarcity, urgency, proof, bonuses and guarantee, say where the copy already does it, quoting the words.
2. **Find what's thin.** The usual gaps: no time frame, no idea of the effort involved, vague outcomes ("clarity", "peace of mind"), generic proof, no reason to act now, nothing that takes away the risk.
3. **Suggest, specific and pushed.** For each: the lever it moves, the exact words and where they go, and one line on why. Go further than feels comfortable; it's easier to cut back than to reach for more.
4. **Write the pushed rewrite.** The whole piece with every suggestion applied, in the same format and length range as the original.
5. **List what to check:** every claim someone has to stand behind, every limit or deadline that isn't confirmed as real, every guarantee, every suggestion that costs the seller's time (with rough hours), every `[N]`, and any quote or name that needs permission.

## Mode 2: build or rework an offer

Run the four steps in [references/prompts.md](references/prompts.md): be the buyer; narrow the market and build two or three offers; break each down into sub-offers with scarcity, urgency, proof, bonuses and a guarantee; then, if wanted, the value ladder. Prices only from the person; gaps stay `[TBD]`.

## Mode 3: headlines and names

One pick and 4 to 6 options in a table, each with the angle it takes, from safe to bold. A headline carries the outcome and, where there is one, the time or deadline. For naming an offer, use MAGIC (in the frameworks file).

## Rules that never move

- **No made-up numbers.** Counts, results, percentages and client outcomes are real or left as `[N]`.
- **No bonus values unless they're real.** A bonus gets a price only if it's what the seller actually charges for it. Otherwise say what it does.
- **Scarcity, urgency and guarantees must be true.** Word real limits hard. Any limit, deadline or guarantee that isn't confirmed goes on the to-check list. Fake urgency is spotted at once, and in some places it's unlawful.
- **Push the substance, not the tone.** No hype words, no shouting, no "irresistible". Plain words.

**Warmth changes the push.** Warm readers (they know you): the person, the relationship and the help do most of the work. Lukewarm: proof and a concrete outcome. Cold: the full stack. Say in one line where the push might feel like too much for this reader.

**Candidates are buyers too:** the dream outcome is the career, the work and the people; likelihood is proof the company will make it; time delay is how soon they'd get real responsibility; effort is what they'd give up. **Partners buy for their members:** their dream outcome is looking good to the people they serve, and their effort is how much organising it costs them.

## Output

In this order:
1. One line on what it is, who it's for and how warm they are.
2. **What already works**, by lever, quoting the copy.
3. **Suggestions**, numbered, with the lever in bold at the start of each.
4. **The pushed rewrite.**
5. **To check.**

## What good looks like

A good run gives suggestions that each name one lever and the exact words, like: "**Time delay.** After 'Join the group', add 'Your first draft goal set in one afternoon.' The copy promises a result but never says how fast." The rewrite is visibly bolder than the original, every number in it is either real or `[N]`, and the to-check list catches every limit and guarantee before anything goes out.

A weak run gives generic advice ("add urgency", "be more specific"), invents figures or bonus values, or writes hype the reader would see through.
