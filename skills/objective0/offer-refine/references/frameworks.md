# The frameworks

A digest of Alex Hormozi's offer methods, from book summaries, Hormozi's podcast and Acquisition.com's free Offers course outline (sources at the end). It hasn't been checked line by line against the books, and points only one source covered are marked **[check]**. Use the thinking and leave the hype behind.

## 1. The value equation

Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷ (Time delay × Effort and sacrifice)

Most offers compete on the top half: a bigger promise and more proof. There's usually more room in the bottom half, which is getting the result faster and with less work. A fast result can beat a free one.

**Moves for each lever**

- **Dream outcome (raise it).** Make it specific and measurable: a result, by a time. Say what it means for how the buyer is seen (by their board, their investors, their team), not just what they get.
- **Perceived likelihood (raise it).** Proof that it works *for someone like them*: real numbers, named methods, results, quotes, a guarantee, and being clear about what's included and what isn't.
- **Time delay (cut it).** A first visible win within days, milestones inside anything long, and saying when they'll see each one.
- **Effort and sacrifice (cut it).** List everything the buyer has to give: hours, a blank page, chasing their team, embarrassment, risk. Then remove what you can. Done-for-you cuts effort most and costs the seller most.


## 2. Market

- **The market beats the offer, and the offer beats the copy.** A great offer to the wrong crowd still fails.
- **Four signs of a good market:** lots of pain (the most important), money to spend, easy to find in one place, and growing.
- **Narrow the market and the price can go up.** The same material is worth far more to a tightly defined group than to everyone. Don't keep switching markets: each switch starts the learning again.
- **Commoditisation.** If buyers can compare you like for like, they choose on price. The way out is an offer nobody else has in that shape.


## 3. Price

- **Raise the value, then the price.** Don't discount the core offer; add bonuses instead. Discounts teach buyers that the price can be negotiated.
- **The virtuous cycle.** A higher price brings more committed buyers, who put in more and get better results. That gives stronger proof and more margin to spend on delivery. A low price runs the cycle backwards.
- **The gap.** People buy when the value they see clearly beats the price. The whole offer exists to widen that gap.


## 4. Building the offer

1. **The dream outcome,** specific and measurable.
2. **Every problem.** Walk through what the buyer does before, during and after, and list every obstacle without filtering. A useful prompt for each lever **[check]**:
   - "It won't work because…"
   - "It won't work for me because…"
   - "It'll take too long because…"
   - "It'll be too hard because…"

   Problems that come *after* often become the next offer.
3. **Turn each problem into a solution,** phrased as "how to…" and given a name.
4. **Choose how to deliver each one.** The options:
   - **Attention:** one-to-one, small group, or one-to-many.
   - **Effort:** they do it, we do it with them, or we do it for them.
   - **Medium:** in person, call, video, written, chat.
   - **Response speed.**

   Ask what you'd deliver at ten times the price, and what would still work at a tenth.
5. **Trim and stack.** Score each item on value to them and cost to you. Cut what costs a lot and gives little, then what costs little and gives little. Keep everything high-value. Bundle the rest into a few named packages: problem, solution, name, then what's in it.

Go wide first, then cut hard (divergent, then convergent).


## 5. Making it harder to say no

**Scarcity: a limit on how many.** Seats, slots, bonuses for the first N, or never offered again. For a service the honest limits are capacity (hours, clients a month) and cohort size. Real limits can be worded hard; any limit that isn't confirmed goes on the to-check list.

**Urgency: a limit on time.** Four honest kinds:
- **Cohorts** that close until the next start date.
- **The season,** with a real end (planning next year's goals in December).
- **A price or bonus that genuinely changes** on a date.
- **An advantage that shrinks with waiting,** such as being early to something.

**Bonuses.**
- Name each one for the benefit it gives.
- Say what it is, which problem or objection it removes, and how it makes things faster or easier.
- Tools and templates beat more training.
- The best bonus solves the next problem the buyer will hit.
- Bonuses can carry their own scarcity (for founding members only).
- Hormozi gives every bonus a price. Only do that when the price is real (see section 8).

**Guarantees.** Removing risk tackles the biggest objection. Four kinds:
- **Unconditional:** a refund if they're unhappy.
- **Conditional:** "do X, and if you don't get Y, we keep going for free". The conditions are the things that make it work, so the guarantee doubles as a commitment.
- **Anti-guarantee:** all sales final, with the reason given. This suits things that can't be returned once seen, such as templates.
- **Performance:** paid on results.

They can be stacked. Any guarantee suggested needs the people who'd honour it to agree before it's used.

**Naming: MAGIC.** Use 3 to 5 of the five parts, and keep the name as short as it can be.
- **M, magnetic reason why now:** a season, a launch, founding.
- **A, avatar:** who it's for, which leaves others out on purpose.
- **G, goal:** the outcome.
- **I, interval:** how long it takes (90 days, an afternoon).
- **C, container word:** sprint, intensive, day, kit, room. Avoid the shouty ones.


## 6. Checklist for reviewing copy

- Is the outcome specific: who, what result, by when?
- Is there proof next to the claim, and is it real?
- Can the reader see how fast the first win comes?
- Can they see what they *don't* have to do?
- Is the risk removed, and is that easy to see?
- Does each bonus remove a named objection, or solve the next problem, rather than pad the list?
- Could a buyer line this up against a competitor on price alone?
- Does the headline carry the outcome? Hormozi tests the headline first, because it does most of the work.
- Is there a real limit behind every scarcity and urgency line?
- The bar: would saying no feel like a mistake to this reader?

## 7. From $100M Leads and $100M Money Models

**Lead magnets.** A free thing that solves one narrow problem and, once solved, shows the next problem, which the paid offer solves. It should be good enough to charge for. Three kinds:
- **Reveal a problem:** an audit, diagnostic or scorecard.
- **A sample or trial.**
- **One step of a multi-step process,** free.

Give away the how, and sell the doing.


**Channels.** Warm outreach, posting content, cold outreach and paid ads. Warm comes first. In warm messages, acknowledge what they said, say something specific and genuine, then ask a question. Asking "do you know anyone who…?" takes the pressure off. Lead with what's in it for them.

**The ladder** (Money Models):
- **The order:** an entry offer, then an upsell or downsell, then something recurring.
- **Upsells** solve the next problem the first purchase reveals.
- **Downsells** change the terms or features, never the price of the same thing.
- **Recurring offers** earn their keep through commitment: a longer term gets a better deal.
- **Build one stage at a time.**


## 8. Where it goes wrong, and the rules

- **Fake scarcity and urgency.** Hormozi says they must be real, but they're the most abused tactics. In the UK the CMA treats false urgency in consumer sales as unlawful (the Emma Sleep countdown-timer case). Founders spot them at once. **The rule:** suggestions can push on limits and deadlines, but any that isn't confirmed as real goes on the to-check list, and nothing goes out until someone has confirmed it.
- **Inflated bonus values.** "£12,000 of value for free" is the most obvious hype tell. **The rule:** a value only if it's what you actually charge. Otherwise say what the bonus does.
- **Hype names.** MAGIC can produce "88% Off 12-Week Blueprint". **The rule:** keep the audience, outcome and time; drop the gimmick.
- **Guarantees as theatre.** Conditions nobody could meet are bait. **The rule:** suggest any kind, with conditions that are the real success behaviours, and flag each one for the people who'd honour it.
- **The method's limits.** It's built mostly on consumer and local-service examples, and it treats buying as value against price. Founders also weigh their time against everything else competing for it. For busy B2B buyers, specific proof and less effort usually do more than scarcity and urgency.
